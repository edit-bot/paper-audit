# Research Specificity Rules

## Principle

Academic prose is strongest when claims are proportionate to what the study actually did and observed.

The central question is:

> Could this sentence have been written without conducting this study?

This is a diagnostic question, not a deletion rule.

## A. Study-dependent sentences

These usually deserve preservation.

Examples of study dependence:

- `Of the 80 transfer pathways identified, 11 required a formal inter-institutional handover.`
- `Seven of the twelve documents explicitly distinguished attendance recognition from academic assessment.`
- `The 2019 guidance added a formal reporting interface that was absent from the 2016 version.`

Signals:

- study-generated counts or categories;
- document-specific chronology;
- named policy or institutional rules;
- observed mechanisms;
- direct comparisons;
- empirical exceptions;
- source-specific wording.

### Audit questions

1. Is the claim directly supported by the study material?
2. Is the level of certainty appropriate?
3. Is an observed pattern being described before it is interpreted?
4. Could a reader trace the claim to data, documents, or analysis?

## B. Concept-dependent sentences

These can be essential, especially in theory, literature review, and discussion.

Examples:

- `Responsibility completeness refers to whether all functions required to sustain a pathway are institutionally assigned.`
- `This distinction separates formal availability from practical usability.`

### Audit questions

1. Is the concept defined?
2. Is it attributed where necessary?
3. Does nearby evidence show why the concept matters here?
4. Does the concept clarify the data rather than merely rename it?
5. Is the concept used consistently?

### Common failure

A concept is introduced elegantly but never operationalised against documents, cases, observations, or data.

Action: **GROUND**.

## C. Rhetoric-dependent sentences

These are not automatically bad. Strategic rhetoric can improve readability. The problem is when rhetoric substitutes for analysis.

Examples:

- `This shift reveals a broader transformation in the institutional boundaries of schooling.`
- `What matters, therefore, is not access alone but the architecture of responsibility.`
- `The significance of this change extends far beyond the immediate policy context.`

### Audit questions

1. What new information does the sentence add?
2. Could the sentence appear unchanged in a different paper?
3. Does the previous sentence already establish the point?
4. Can the claim be replaced by a concrete study-specific observation?
5. Is the sentence functioning mainly as a memorable line?

Action: usually **REWRITE** or **DELETE**.

## Exceptions

Do not penalize non-study-specific language when it is performing a legitimate function, including:

- defining a construct;
- stating a research question;
- reporting a method;
- summarizing established literature;
- identifying a limitation;
- explaining a standard statistical or analytical procedure;
- giving necessary institutional background.

## Gap claims

Treat claims such as the following as high-risk unless demonstrated:

- `Few studies have examined...`
- `Little attention has been paid to...`
- `This issue remains underexplored...`
- `No previous research has...`

A gap claim should normally be supported by a transparent account of what neighbouring literatures have and have not done.

## Contribution claims

Prefer contributions that name the actual analytical advance.

Weak:

- `This study contributes to the growing literature on educational inequality.`

Stronger:

- `The study distinguishes formal route availability from the institutional assignment of responsibility for re-entry, allowing the two to be analysed separately.`

The stronger form is not preferred because it sounds less like AI; it is preferred because it specifies the contribution.
