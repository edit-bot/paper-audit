# Precision and Readability Rules

## Principle

Clarity should preserve disciplinary precision. Do not simplify a sentence merely to make it shorter.

## 1. Agents and responsibility

Clarify the actor when agency matters.

Weak:

- `Support was provided when needed.`

Questions:

- By whom?
- Under what rule?
- Who determines need?

However, passive voice is appropriate when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or deliberately backgrounded.

## 2. Inanimate subjects

Do not automatically remove inanimate subjects.

Legitimate academic examples:

- `The policy permits...`
- `The analysis shows...`
- `The dataset contains...`
- `The regulation requires...`

Flag only when agency is falsely implied or the construction obscures responsibility.

## 3. Referents

Check `this`, `these`, `it`, `they`, `such`, and `the former/latter` when the referent could be ambiguous.

Prefer a noun phrase when ambiguity matters.

## 4. Abstract nouns

Nominalisation is normal in academic writing. Flag only when several abstract nouns obscure the underlying process.

Example:

- `The institutionalisation of responsibility allocation produced a reconfiguration of continuity provision.`

Ask what institution did what, to which responsibility, and with what observable change.

## 5. Verb precision

Prefer verbs that describe an observable or institutional action.

Potentially vague:

- `shapes`;
- `drives`;
- `transforms`;
- `reconfigures`;
- `enables`;
- `undermines`.

These are not forbidden. They require a clear object and, for causal verbs, adequate evidence.

## 6. Causal language

Distinguish:

- caused;
- contributed to;
- was associated with;
- coincided with;
- followed;
- was consistent with.

Do not upgrade descriptive evidence into causal language during editing.

## 7. Sentence length

No fixed word or character target.

Flag a sentence when:

- it contains multiple independent claims;
- the main verb is difficult to locate;
- qualifications separate subject and predicate excessively;
- citation clusters obscure the claim;
- the reader must retain too many nested contrasts.

## 8. Information density

A long sentence can be clear if each clause adds necessary information. A short sentence can be weak if it contains only generic emphasis.

Audit information gain rather than length alone.

## 9. Repetition

Distinguish useful terminological consistency from avoidable rhetorical repetition.

Do not vary a technical term merely to avoid repetition if synonyms would reduce conceptual precision.

## 10. Paragraph structure

Do not require every paragraph to follow a rigid topic-sentence / evidence / implication template.

Prefer paragraph structure that matches the analytical task:

- claim → evidence → qualification;
- chronology;
- comparison;
- mechanism;
- source synthesis;
- definition → application.
