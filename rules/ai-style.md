# AI-Style Pattern Rules

## Scope

These rules identify stylistic patterns that can make academic prose sound generic, over-regular, or rhetorically over-engineered.

They do **not** identify AI authorship.

## Governing rule

> Pattern alone is not sufficient for revision.

Flag only when a pattern is generic, repetitive, weakly grounded, rhetorically unnecessary, or associated with low information gain.

## 1. Contrast templates

Watch for repeated forms such as:

- `not X but Y`;
- `rather than X, Y`;
- `the question is not whether X, but how Y`;
- `X is not the problem; Y is`;
- `not merely X, but Y`.

A single contrast can be effective. Repeated contrastive architecture can make prose feel manufactured and can compress empirical differences into slogans.

### Do not flag when

- the contrast is analytically necessary;
- the two categories are explicitly defined;
- the distinction is used consistently in the analysis.

## 2. Generic significance pivots

Examples:

- `This highlights...`
- `This reveals...`
- `This underscores...`
- `This points to...`
- `This speaks to...`
- `What matters is...`
- `The key point is...`
- `More broadly...`
- `Taken together, these findings suggest...`

These are legitimate academic transitions. Treat an isolated occurrence as a Low-level review candidate when useful, and escalate only when the clause is generic, weakly grounded, or the same staging recurs locally.

## 3. Broad abstract implication language

Potential signals:

- `broader implications`;
- `complex interplay`;
- `multifaceted nature`;
- `dynamic landscape`;
- `important role`;
- `critical importance`;
- `nuanced understanding`;
- `meaningful change`;
- `wider transformation`.

Ask: implications for what, interplay between which variables or institutions, important in what observable way?

## 4. One-line pivots

A short standalone sentence can be effective in essays but may look rhetorically staged in academic prose.

Examples:

- `Their value lies elsewhere.`
- `The problem is one of connection.`
- `Recognition is not responsibility.`

Flag when the line functions as a slogan and the analytical content is supplied only afterward.

Do not flag headings, explicit propositions, or carefully defined conceptual distinctions merely because they are short.

## 4A. Metadiscursive conceptual staging

Concept-heavy papers often need to distinguish neighbouring ideas explicitly. Phrases such as the following are legitimate in isolation:

- `The important point is...`
- `The relevant question is...`
- `The contribution is...`
- `The distinction matters because...`
- `The matrix is not... It is...`

The risk appears when several such pivots occur within a short span and repeatedly tell the reader how to interpret the argument instead of stating the distinction directly.

PaperAudit therefore treats these as **density-sensitive** patterns rather than prohibited phrases.

### Negative redefinition pairs

Repeated forms such as:

- `X is not a taxonomy. It is a diagnostic device.`
- `The contribution is not another scale. It is an account of...`

can create polished conceptual symmetry. Keep a single pair when it defines a necessary boundary. Review repeated, non-overlapping pairs for over-staging or slogan-like compression. Chained sentences that share the same middle sentence should count as one rhetorical move, not two repetitions.


## 4B. Compact antithesis pairs

Short two-sentence reversals can be effective:

- `Responsibility may move. It should not disappear.`
- `The final destination is similar. The institutional pathway is not.`
- `Distributed provision is not the problem. Fragmented responsibility is.`

Do not treat one pair as a defect. Review the manuscript when this architecture recurs repeatedly, because accumulated aphoristic staging can make the prose feel over-engineered even when each distinction is defensible.

## 4C. Exact sentence repetition

Conceptual papers sometimes repeat a diagnostic question, proposition, or refrain intentionally. Surface exact sentence repetition as a **Low-level review candidate**, not as an error.

Ask whether each recurrence:

- marks a deliberate analytical checkpoint;
- performs a different local function; or
- merely restates a memorable line.

Repeated diagnostic questions may be useful. Repeated slogans or conclusions may be compressible.


## 5. Three-part symmetry

Repeated triads can produce artificial regularity:

- three adjectives;
- three consequences;
- three parallel clauses;
- three-item lists in consecutive paragraphs.

Do not penalize a genuine three-category empirical classification.

## 6. Repeated sentence architecture

Examples:

- many consecutive sentences beginning `This study...`;
- repeated `By doing X, Y...` constructions;
- repeated `While X, Y...` openings;
- multiple paragraph endings with `This suggests that...`.

The problem is not the construction itself but its density.

## 7. Formulaic paragraph closure

Flag paragraphs that repeatedly end by escalating significance:

- evidence → interpretation → `broader implication`;
- example → explanation → `This highlights the importance of...`;
- literature → synthesis → `This gap underscores the need for...`.

Academic paragraphs do not all need a miniature conclusion.

## 8. Excessive signposting

Potentially unnecessary:

- `It is important to note that...`;
- `It should be emphasized that...`;
- `It is worth noting that...`;
- `Crucially...`;
- `Importantly...`;
- `Notably...` repeated frequently.

If the claim is important, the evidence and placement should often carry the emphasis.

## 9. Over-polished conceptual rhetoric

Flag sentences in which abstract concepts are arranged into highly symmetrical or aphoristic forms while empirical content is absent.

Example:

- `Flexibility expands opportunity while simultaneously redistributing the burden of coordination.`

This may be a strong conceptual sentence. Keep it if the manuscript subsequently demonstrates both propositions. Ground or revise it if it functions only as a polished generalization.

## 10. Vocabulary is secondary

Do not maintain a simplistic blacklist of "AI words". Terms such as `critical`, `complex`, `robust`, `nuanced`, or `significant` may be appropriate.

Audit their function:

- Does the adjective name a measurable or defined property?
- Is it repeated?
- Would the sentence lose no information if it were removed?

## Protected academic conventions

Do not flag these merely because they are common:

- `This study examines...`
- `This paper argues...`
- `Previous research has shown...`
- `The findings suggest...`
- `The analysis indicates...`
- `A limitation of this study is...`
- `The results are consistent with...`
- `Further research is needed...`

They may still be revised for repetition, vagueness, or evidentiary overreach.

## Risk rating

### Low

Conventional academic language, varied syntax, strong study-specific content, and limited generic rhetoric.

### Moderate

Several recurring generic pivots, repeated templates, or polished abstractions, but substantive evidence remains visible.

### High

Generic significance language, symmetrical rhetoric, repeated templates, and abstract interpretation repeatedly displace study-specific observations.
